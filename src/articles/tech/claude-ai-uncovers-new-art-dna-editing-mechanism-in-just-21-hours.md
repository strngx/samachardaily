Anthropic's Claude AI has identified a previously unknown enzyme system called ART, which the company suspects could point to a new DNA editing mechanism, after a 21-hour automated search through genetic databases. The finding was announced on Wednesday, 23 September 2026, and its precise function is still not known.

Anthropic published the result in a blog post and a technical preprint, a research paper that has not yet been through peer review. It is the first public result from a biology laboratory the company set up in spring 2026. The system, named array-associated reverse transcriptases, or ART, was found in bacteriophages, the viruses that infect bacteria.

## What Happened: Claude's 21-Hour Search

According to Anthropic, its scientists gave Claude a single high-level prompt: search a very large database of DNA sequences for interesting new examples of reverse transcriptases, which are enzymes that copy RNA into DNA. The company says its own involvement was limited to that prompt and, later, the laboratory work. The AI agents did the database analysis, investigated different enzyme families and decided which candidates deserved a closer look.

Roughly 950 Claude agents worked in parallel for 21 hours and used about 210 million tokens, the small units of text that an AI model reads and writes as it works. Anthropic says the agents gathered more than 200,000 reverse transcriptases, picked out 3,500 new candidate systems and narrowed those to the 20 most compelling for detailed written reports. The company says a comparable analysis could take an expert scientist weeks to months.

One agent then noticed something unusual beside the gene for an odd-looking reverse transcriptase: a repeating pattern of DNA sequences. According to Anthropic, the agent counted the repeats, measured their spacing, compared the layout with known systems and searched the scientific literature for earlier reports of the pattern. Only then did it file a report for human review.

## What ART Is and Why It Resembles CRISPR

Anthropic describes ART as a system with three parts: a reverse transcriptase, a partner gene next to it, and a long array of evenly spaced, repeated DNA sequences. The repeat layout resembles a CRISPR array, the stretch of bacterial DNA that holds a bank of RNA sequences and makes CRISPR-Cas systems programmable. Early laboratory experiments showed that the ART array is read out as a set of distinct short RNAs, which Anthropic says suggests that something analogous to CRISPR may be at work.

The company says this combination of features has only ever been found together in a handful of other systems, all of them programmable and able to cut, copy or paste DNA. Reports on the preprint say the arrays hold between three and 21 copies of a short repeat.

Anthropic is careful about what is new here. The underlying reverse transcriptase, found in a jumbo phage, had been identified in earlier studies. What Claude appears to have been the first to notice, the company says, is the associated repeat array and an accessory protein of unknown function, which together may form a distinct system.

## Background: From Bacterial Defence to Gene-Editing Tool

CRISPR began as a bacterial defence mechanism against invading viruses before scientists adapted it into a gene-editing technology. Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna received the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for pioneering its use to edit DNA in living organisms. The technology has since been credited with advancing treatments for conditions including sickle cell disease and cancer.

Anthropic points to a wider pattern in the history of biotechnology. Restriction enzymes, found in bacterial immune systems, helped launch the biotechnology industry. Taq polymerase, taken from a bacterium in a Yellowstone hot spring, became the basis of the PCR method used in much diagnostic testing. CRISPR itself was first noticed as an unusual repeat sequence in bacterial DNA. The company's argument is that spotting odd patterns in natural DNA has repeatedly led to major tools.

## Who Is Behind the Work

The research comes from a new life sciences group formed in spring 2026 to test whether general AI models can speed up discoveries of this kind. Its laboratory in the Bay Area works only at biosafety levels BSL-1 and BSL-2 and does not handle pathogens that can infect humans, Anthropic says. All laboratory work is done by human scientists, while Claude helps interpret the data. The team says it works in Claude Science and Claude Code, tools available to other researchers.

Anthropic also describes a routine in which Claude reads the relevant literature, reproduces established results from public data to check its own methods, proposes a function for each candidate and then critically evaluates the evidence. Most candidates are eliminated at that stage, and a survey may end with a single candidate worth testing or with none.

The company says it chose to share the early result both to demonstrate what Claude can do and to give the wider scientific community a view of its work. It has invited other scientists to propose research questions.

## Reactions From Scientists

Feng Zhang, a CRISPR pioneer at MIT and the Broad Institute, reviewed the preprint and called it an exciting example of how AI agents can contribute to biological discovery, according to Anthropic's announcement. He described the identification of RNA-repeat arrays linked to reverse transcriptases as:

> genuinely intriguing and merits further investigation

Stanley Qi, an associate professor of bioengineering at Stanford University, told Al Jazeera that he found the result highly exciting. He pointed to the system's ability to recognise a biological pattern that was hard to detect before and to pursue it as a research question.

Kevin Blake, a microbiologist at Washington University School of Medicine, was sceptical about its significance. He told Al Jazeera that a CRISPR-like sequence should not be confused with CRISPR as a technology, and that many such sequences remain uncatalogued because millions of bacterial species are still unstudied. In his view, nothing yet indicates a rival to CRISPR or a therapeutic application.

Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei, who has said AI could cure most diseases within five to 10 years, wrote on X that the system's function, biotechnological usefulness and significance are not yet clear. According to Decrypt, he also said the work was at least something he would have been proud of as a PhD student, a comparison that some commenters on social media disputed.

## Market and Industry Impact

Financial markets reacted quickly. Decrypt, citing Seeking Alpha, reported that shares of gene-editing companies including CRISPR Therapeutics, Intellia Therapeutics and Beam Therapeutics dipped on the news. Anthropic has not claimed that ART is a usable tool, and the same report notes that any useful application would still be years away from reaching patients. This article is not investment advice.

## What It Means for India

The announcement has no direct Indian connection, but it arrives as India builds its own gene-editing capacity. In November 2025 India launched BIRSA 101, which it describes as its first indigenous CRISPR-based gene therapy for sickle cell disease, developed by the CSIR-Institute of Genomics and Integrative Biology (CSIR-IGIB). The disease disproportionately affects tribal communities, and a technology transfer agreement with the Serum Institute of India aims to scale the therapy affordably. The institute's website lists a Phase-I trial as expected in 2027.

BIRSA 101 is built on an engineered CRISPR-Cas9 platform known as enFnCas9 and has no link to ART, which remains at the stage of basic research. The two stories show how far apart the stages of gene-editing work can be: one therapy is moving towards clinical trials, while the other is still a pattern in viral DNA.

## What We Do Not Yet Know

Anthropic says its work to understand ART's primary function is ongoing. It is not yet known whether the system cuts, copies or pastes DNA, what role it plays in the phages that carry it, or whether it could ever be developed into a laboratory or medical tool. The results come from Anthropic's own preprint and have not been independently replicated or peer reviewed.

Commentators have also pointed out that humans wrote the initial prompt, reviewed the candidate reports and carried out all of the laboratory experiments, which shapes how the word "autonomous" should be read. Anthropic itself says the lab work was done by its human scientists.

As of 5 October 2026, the sources reviewed report no confirmed function for ART and no peer-reviewed results. For now, the Claude AI ART DNA editing mechanism is best understood as an intriguing, early-stage lead: a new arrangement of known biological parts that Anthropic's own scientists say they are still working to explain.
