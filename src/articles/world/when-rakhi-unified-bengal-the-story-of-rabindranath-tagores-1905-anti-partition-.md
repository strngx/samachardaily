At the turn of the twentieth century, the British administration under Lord Curzon announced the partition of Bengal, carving the province into a Muslim‑majority East and a Hindu‑majority West. The move was widely perceived as a classic "divide‑and‑rule" tactic, sparking a wave of nationalist agitation that would later coalesce into the Swadeshi movement. Amidst this ferment, Rabindranath Tagore, already renowned for his literary brilliance, turned to cultural symbolism to rally the masses. He invoked Raksha Bandhan—a festival where sisters tie protective threads on their brothers—as a metaphor for communal solidarity. Tagore urged both Hindus and Muslims to exchange rakhis, thereby transforming a familial ritual into a public declaration of brotherhood against the colonial split.

Tagore’s call resonated in the streets of Calcutta and the villages of rural Bengal. Groups of students, teachers, and ordinary citizens gathered on the auspicious day of Raksha Bandhan, tying rakhis across religious lines and pledging to defend each other's rights. Contemporary newspapers reported that the gatherings were marked by songs, poems, and Tagore’s own verses that celebrated unity. While the British authorities dismissed the act as a harmless cultural display, the symbolic act of shared rakhi quickly became a rallying point for the anti‑partition protests, reinforcing the broader Swadeshi boycott of British goods and institutions. The movement, though ultimately unable to prevent the formal partition, sowed the seeds of a pan‑Bengali identity that transcended communal boundaries.
