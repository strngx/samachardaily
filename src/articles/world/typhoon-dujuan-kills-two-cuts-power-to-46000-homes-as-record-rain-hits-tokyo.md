Severe tropical storm Typhoon Dujuan has killed two people and knocked electrical power out for approximately 46,000 households across eastern Japan, according to municipal authorities and disaster management officials cited by Channel NewsAsia. Although the cyclone weakened as it tracked northeastward toward the Pacific Ocean, its trailing rainbands unleashed record-breaking precipitation across the densely populated Tokyo metropolitan area and neighboring Kanto prefectures.

## Fatalities and emergency response

Municipal police and emergency management departments confirmed two fatalities linked directly to storm conditions. Emergency medical personnel and local fire services responded to hundreds of distress calls as localized flash flooding submerged roadways, flooded basement structures, and compromised residential properties in low-lying suburban wards.

Prefectural civil defense agencies activated emergency coordination centers to monitor rising water levels along metropolitan river basins. Search-and-rescue teams were deployed to assist residents trapped by inundated streets and ensure vulnerable elderly citizens in flood-prone districts were relocated to community shelters.

## Grid disruption and infrastructure impact

High-velocity wind gusts accompanying Dujuan caused widespread damage to regional electrical distribution infrastructure. Flying debris and fallen trees downed overhead utility lines, triggering localized power cuts that left roughly 46,000 homes and commercial facilities without electricity.

Utility teams from the Tokyo Electric Power Company (TEPCO) mobilized emergency repair crews to restore substations and reconnect downed lines. Technicians prioritized critical facilities, including hospitals, transit hubs, and drainage pumping stations, working through adverse weather conditions to stabilize the regional power grid.

The combination of electrical failures and torrential rainfall severely disrupted the capital's commuter transit network. Rail operators across eastern Japan implemented emergency speed restrictions and suspended select suburban train lines to inspect track beds for debris and water accumulation, leaving thousands of commuters facing delays across Tokyo's central rail interchanges.

## Meteorological tracking and urban drainage challenges

Official bulletins released by the Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA) indicated that while Dujuan's central atmospheric pressure weakened following landfall, tropical moisture feeding into the storm system generated intense convective precipitation bands. JMA meteorological stations across metropolitan Tokyo recorded unprecedented localized hourly rainfall rates, prompting agency meteorologists to issue high-level flood and sediment disaster advisories for Tokyo, Kanagawa, Saitama, and Chiba prefectures.

Urban planning experts noted that the intense downpours tested the limits of the capital's complex flood defense networks, including underground diversion channels and retention basins. While subterranean surge reservoirs absorbed millions of cubic meters of runoff from swollen urban rivers, surface drainage systems in densely built commercial districts struggled to cope with the sheer volume of precipitation falling within compressed time windows.

## Official advisories and recovery efforts

Municipal authorities in Tokyo urged residents in vulnerable districts to exercise extreme caution against residual slope instability and ground-water saturation, warning that saturated soil structures remain susceptible to landslides even as precipitation subsides.

Disaster management officials confirmed that cleanup operations and damage assessments are underway across affected prefectures. Municipal works departments deployed industrial pumping units to clear flooded underpasses and arterial roads, while utility providers continued round-the-clock restoration efforts to re-establish electrical service to all remaining disconnected households.
