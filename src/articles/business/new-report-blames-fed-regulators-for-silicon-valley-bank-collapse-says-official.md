A newly released internal regulatory review, detailed in reports by the Wall Street Journal, attributes the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank directly to institutional shortcomings within the Federal Reserve’s own supervisory framework, according to a Fed official familiar with the findings.

The analysis reveals that federal bank examiners identified significant interest-rate risk and liquidity vulnerabilities across SVB’s unhedged held-to-maturity bond portfolio well before the institution's failure, but supervisory teams repeatedly hesitated to escalate informal warnings into binding enforcement orders. The report notes that supervisory staff deferred taking aggressive corrective action partly out of concern over challenging the bank's internal models without definitive consensus.

Financial reporting across CNBC, CNN Business, Yahoo Finance, and Axios highlighted that while SVB’s executive management failed to manage basic duration risks as interest rates rose, the central bank's regional oversight mechanism failed to enforce mandatory capital buffers. Supervisors issued multiple supervisory findings but did not compel the bank to raise immediate equity or restructure its concentrated uninsured deposit base.

The findings have intensified scrutiny over the Federal Reserve's supervisory culture, adding momentum to federal policy proposals aimed at replacing slow consensus-based oversight with automated, mandatory supervisory escalation when mid-sized lenders exhibit severe asset-liability mismatches.
