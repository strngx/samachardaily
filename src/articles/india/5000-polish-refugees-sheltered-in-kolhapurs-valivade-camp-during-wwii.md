During World War Two, a refugee camp in the western Indian principality of Kolhapur became an unexpected haven for thousands of Poles displaced by the European conflict. The Valivade camp, situated in the princely state now part of Maharashtra, sheltered around 5,000 Polish refugees who had fled persecution and hardship after Germany's invasion of Poland in 1939 and the subsequent Soviet occupation of large parts of the country.

The camp quickly earned the moniker 'Little Poland' from its Polish residents, who established schools, churches, cultural associations and even a Polish-language press during their years of displacement. Many of the refugees were women and children who had been deported to forced-labour camps in Soviet Central Asia and were released under a 1941 amnesty following the German-Soviet invasion that reshaped the wartime map.

Their journey from Soviet labour settlements in places like Siberia and Kazakhstan to India's western coast was undertaken via long, arduous overland and sea routes, with many travelling through Iran and then onward to the British Indian subcontinent. The Kolhapur princely state, under the reign of Maharaja Shahu Chhatrapati at the time, agreed to host the arrivals alongside British colonial authorities, providing land and logistical support for the camp's establishment near Valivade village.

At its peak, the Valivade settlement functioned as a self-contained community with its own orphanage that became famous for housing a significant number of Polish children. The camp operated for several years during and after the war, with many residents eventually emigrating to countries such as Australia, Canada, the United Kingdom and the United States once the conflict ended and Poland fell under Soviet influence.

The episode remains one of the lesser-known but enduring chapters of India's wartime humanitarian history, and descendants of the Valivade refugees continue to honour the connection through reunions, memorial visits and cultural exchanges with Kolhapur. A small cemetery and museum at the site preserve the memory of the years when 'Little Poland' lived on Indian soil.
