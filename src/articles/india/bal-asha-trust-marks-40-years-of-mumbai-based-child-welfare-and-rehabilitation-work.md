Bal Asha Trust, one of Mumbai's longest-running child welfare organisations, has completed 40 years of caring for abandoned and vulnerable children. Founded in the mid-1980s, the non-governmental organisation has built its work around three core pillars: rescue and rehabilitation of children in distress, family reunification wherever possible, and legal adoption when biological families cannot be traced or are unable to care for the child.

Operating out of Mumbai, the trust runs shelter homes and rehabilitation programmes designed to provide a stable environment for children who would otherwise face institutional neglect, trafficking risks, or life on the streets. Staff and caregivers work with child welfare committees, juvenile justice boards, and state authorities to ensure every child's case is processed under India's adoption and child protection laws. The legal adoption component is conducted in coordination with the Central Adoption Resource Authority (CARA), the statutory body that regulates in-country and inter-country adoptions.

Beyond shelter, Bal Asha Trust runs outreach programmes aimed at preventing family separation in the first place. Counsellors work with parents facing economic or social crises, providing support that allows children to remain with their biological families when reunification is in the child's best interest. When children are found abandoned in hospitals, railway stations, or public places, the trust's rescue teams intervene promptly to bring them into safe care.

The milestone comes at a time when Indian child welfare organisations are grappling with rising reports of abandonment in urban centres and a persistent gap between the number of children awaiting adoption and the pool of prospective parents. Bal Asha's four-decade record places it alongside a small group of legacy NGOs that have shaped the country's adoption and child rights ecosystem. The organisation says it will continue to expand its reunification-first approach, while maintaining its adoption services for children whose families cannot be located.
