Speaking at a legal forum in London, Chief Justice of India Surya Kant said the nation’s courts must move proactively to address the surge in digital arrest scams, a form of fraud that exploits the fear of police action to extort money from victims. He emphasized that waiting for Parliament to enact new statutes would leave citizens vulnerable to increasingly sophisticated online deceptions.

Digital arrest scams typically involve fraudsters impersonating law‑enforcement officials via phone, email or messaging apps, claiming that the target is under investigation and demanding immediate payment of fines or bail. Over the past year, complaints to cyber‑crime cells have risen sharply, prompting concerns that the existing legal framework is ill‑equipped to deter or punish perpetrators swiftly.

Historically, the Indian Parliament has been the primary engine for criminal law reform, drafting statutes that define offences and prescribe penalties. The CJI’s remarks signal a shift toward judicial activism, suggesting that courts can issue interim orders, set procedural guidelines, and even direct law‑enforcement agencies to adopt preventive measures while legislative processes lag.

The Supreme Court has previously exercised suo motu jurisdiction in matters of public interest, issuing directives on issues ranging from consumer protection to data privacy. Kant indicated that a similar approach could be adopted for digital arrest scams, including fast‑track hearings, the establishment of dedicated cyber‑crime benches, and the issuance of standard operating procedures for police and prosecutors handling such cases.

By urging immediate judicial response, the CJI aims to create a deterrent effect and provide relief to victims before comprehensive legislation is passed. His call also underscores the need for coordination between the judiciary, law‑enforcement agencies, and the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology to develop a cohesive strategy against this evolving threat.
