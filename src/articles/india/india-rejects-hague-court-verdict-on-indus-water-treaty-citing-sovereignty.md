New Delhi announced on Thursday that it will not accept the Hague court's recent verdict concerning the Indus Water Treaty, a bilateral accord that governs the sharing of river waters between India and its neighbour. The Ministry of External Affairs issued a brief statement rejecting the judgment, describing it as an intrusion into India’s sovereign right to manage its own water resources.

The Hague court ruling, delivered after a petition filed by an environmental coalition, called for a re‑examination of water allocations under the treaty, arguing that the existing framework fails to reflect contemporary ecological realities. India’s response emphasized that the treaty, negotiated decades ago, remains a legally binding instrument that has provided a stable mechanism for water distribution, and that any amendment must arise from direct bilateral talks, not external adjudication.

Officials noted that the decision does not alter the operational arrangements already in place on the Indus river system, where India releases water according to the schedule agreed upon in the treaty. However, they warned that the court’s intervention could set a precedent for third‑party bodies to intervene in bilateral agreements, a prospect that New Delhi finds unacceptable. The statement also highlighted that India will continue to honour its obligations while seeking a diplomatic resolution to the concerns raised.

Analysts point out that the rejection may heighten tensions between the two countries, as water scarcity has increasingly become a flashpoint in their relationship. The treaty has historically been a cornerstone of cooperation, but recent climate‑induced fluctuations in river flow have revived debates over equitable sharing. By refusing to implement the Hague court’s recommendations, India signals its intent to keep negotiations strictly bilateral, potentially limiting the scope for international mediation.

The development arrives at a time when both nations are grappling with broader water‑security challenges across the subcontinent. While the immediate impact on water deliveries is expected to be minimal, the diplomatic fallout could influence future talks on related issues such as hydro‑electric projects and flood management. Observers suggest that the next steps will involve high‑level diplomatic channels aimed at defusing the dispute and preserving the functional aspects of the treaty.
